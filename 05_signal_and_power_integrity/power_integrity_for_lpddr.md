# Power Integrity for LPDDR

This section covers power integrity (PI) analysis for LPDDRx interfaces. PI ensures that the power supply voltage at the IO cells remains within the specified tolerance despite the large transient currents generated by high-speed switching.

---

### Q1. What is the VDDQ ripple budget for LPDDR5, and how is it allocated?

**Answer:**

The VDDQ ripple budget defines the maximum allowable voltage deviation from the nominal VDDQ value at the IO cell supply pins. For LPDDR5 at 0.5V VDDQ, the typical ripple budget is 3-5% of VDDQ, which is 15-25 mV. This budget must accommodate DC IR drop (static voltage drop due to resistive losses in the power grid), AC Ldi/dt noise (dynamic voltage drop from inductive effects during current transients), resonance-induced voltage amplification (voltage peaking at PDN resonant frequencies), and regulator output ripple (voltage variation from the PMIC or LDO feeding VDDQ).

The budget allocation typically reserves 5-10 mV for DC IR drop (achievable with a well-designed power grid with multiple bumps and wide straps), 10-15 mV for AC Ldi/dt noise (the dominant contributor, driven by the fast current transients during DQ switching), and 2-5 mV for regulator ripple (depends on the PMIC or LDO design). The total must not exceed 15-25 mV.

VDDQ ripple directly impacts signal integrity because the driver output voltage swings between VSS and VDDQ. If VDDQ droops by 20 mV during a logic-high output, the received voltage decreases by 20 mV, reducing the eye height. Additionally, VDDQ noise couples into the VREF generator (if VREF is derived from VDDQ), shifting the receiver threshold and further reducing the effective margin. VDDQ noise also modulates the PLL/DLL supply (if not independently regulated), creating jitter on the clock signals.

For layout, meeting the VDDQ ripple budget requires a combination of low-resistance power grid (for DC IR drop), low-inductance bump and via connections (for AC Ldi/dt), and strategically placed decoupling capacitance (for absorbing high-frequency current transients). The PI analysis must be performed with accurate current profiles that include worst-case switching patterns.

### Q2. How is PDN impedance analysis performed for the LPDDR PHY?

**Answer:**

PDN impedance analysis determines the impedance seen by the switching circuits looking into the power distribution network at each frequency. The target is to keep this impedance below the target impedance (Z_target = V_noise / I_max) across the frequency range from DC to beyond the data rate.

The analysis flow starts with extracting the on-die power grid from the layout. This produces a resistive and inductive network model of the VDDQ and VSS metal routing, including all straps, vias, and bump connections. Next, the package PDN model is obtained from the package design team, including bump inductance, substrate power plane models, and package capacitor connections. The on-die and package models are combined into a single PDN model.

The PDN impedance is computed by applying a current source at each IO cell location and measuring the voltage response across frequency (using AC analysis in SPICE or a dedicated PDN solver). The impedance Z(f) = V(f) / I(f) is plotted versus frequency. Alternatively, the PDN impedance can be computed using the Z-parameter extraction from the combined RL network.

The impedance plot typically shows several regions. At DC, the impedance is dominated by the metal resistance (a few milliohms to tens of milliohms). At low frequencies (1-100 MHz), the package bulk capacitors reduce the impedance. At mid frequencies (100 MHz - 1 GHz), the impedance is dominated by the package inductance, which increases with frequency. At high frequencies (above 1 GHz), the on-die decoupling capacitors reduce the impedance again.

The critical check is whether the impedance exceeds Z_target at any frequency. If it does, additional decoupling or reduced inductance is needed at that frequency. The resonance peak between the package inductance and on-die capacitance is the most common problem area.

### Q3. What is the role of MIM capacitors in LPDDR power integrity, and how are they placed?

**Answer:**

MIM (Metal-Insulator-Metal) capacitors provide high-density on-die decoupling that is effective at high frequencies (500 MHz to 5+ GHz). They are formed by two metal plates separated by a thin high-k dielectric, inserted between specific metal layers in the back-end-of-line (BEOL) process.

MIM capacitors have several advantages for LPDDR PI. Their high capacitance density (10-20 fF/um^2) allows significant decoupling in a small area. Their low ESR (Equivalent Series Resistance, typically 10-50 mohm) makes them effective at high frequencies. Their low ESL (Equivalent Series Inductance, typically 5-20 pH) ensures they remain capacitive well into the GHz range.

For LPDDR PHY, MIM capacitors are placed between the VDDQ power straps, as close to the IO cells as possible. The ideal placement is directly over the IO cell block, using the metal layers between the IO cell routing and the bump pad layer. This minimises the inductive path from the capacitor to the driver that is drawing current.

The required MIM capacitance depends on the current transient magnitude and the allowable voltage droop. For a current step of 100 mA in 100 ps (typical for 8 DQ pins switching simultaneously) with a voltage droop target of 15 mV:

```
C_required = I * dt / dV = 100 mA * 100 ps / 15 mV = 667 fF
```

This is a minimum for the first current spike. To maintain the voltage during the entire burst (BL16 = 8 CK cycles at 800 MHz = 10 ns), significantly more capacitance is needed:

```
C_sustained = I_avg * t_burst / dV = 50 mA * 10 ns / 15 mV = 33 pF
```

A typical LPDDR5 byte lane uses 100-500 pF of total on-die decoupling, distributed between MIM caps (50-200 pF), MOM caps (20-100 pF), and MOS caps (50-200 pF).

### Q4. How does IR drop affect LPDDR5 signal quality, and how is it analysed?

**Answer:**

IR drop is the DC voltage drop across the resistive power distribution network. For LPDDR5 at 0.5V VDDQ, even a small IR drop is significant: a 10 mV IR drop at the IO cell means the actual VDDQ at the driver is 0.49V, reducing the output voltage swing by 2%.

IR drop affects signal quality through reduced output voltage swing (the driver can only swing to the local VDDQ, which is lower than nominal due to IR drop), shifted VREF (if the VREF generator references local VDDQ, VREF shifts proportionally), and asymmetric switching (PMOS pull-up has less voltage headroom, slowing the rising edge while the falling edge through NMOS is unaffected).

IR drop analysis uses a static (DC) simulation of the power grid. The power grid extracted model (resistive network from layout) is loaded with current sources representing each IO cell's average current draw. The simulator solves the resistive network to find the voltage at every node.

The analysis should be performed for several scenarios. Maximum-current scenario models all DQ drivers active, all ODTs enabled, worst-case data pattern. This produces the highest IR drop and represents the worst-case voltage margin condition. Per-channel scenario models only one channel active, which may produce higher local current density if the power grid is shared across channels. Standby scenario models all channels idle but ODT park termination active, which represents a sustained DC load.

The IR drop results are displayed as a colour map overlaid on the layout, showing the voltage at each point in the grid. Hot spots (areas of excessive IR drop) are identified and addressed by widening power straps in those areas, adding more via connections, increasing the number of VDDQ bumps near the hot spot, or redistributing the current load (changing the IO cell placement).

### Q5. What is Ldi/dt noise, and how is it minimised in the LPDDR PHY layout?

**Answer:**

Ldi/dt noise (also called simultaneous switching noise or SSN) is the voltage perturbation caused by the inductance of the power distribution network when the current changes rapidly. The voltage noise is V = L * di/dt, where L is the effective inductance from the noise-sensitive circuit to the nearest charge storage (decoupling capacitor or regulator output), and di/dt is the rate of current change.

For LPDDR5, the current changes are extremely fast: 8 DQ drivers switching from 0 to VDDQ (or vice versa) in approximately 50-100 ps. The total current step is approximately 100 mA, giving di/dt = 100 mA / 100 ps = 10^9 A/s. Even a small inductance of 50 pH creates V = 50 pH * 10^9 = 50 mV, which is 10% of VDDQ -- exceeding the entire noise budget.

Minimising Ldi/dt noise requires reducing the inductance and reducing the di/dt. Inductance reduction is achieved by using multiple parallel power and ground bumps (each bump has inductance approximately 30-100 pH; N parallel bumps reduce the effective inductance to L/N), by keeping the current loop area small (VDDQ and VSS routing close together on the same or adjacent layers), by using wide metal straps (lower inductance per unit length), and by placing decoupling capacitors close to the drivers (the effective inductance is the path from driver to capacitor, not driver to bump).

Reducing di/dt is achieved through slew rate control on the output drivers (slower transitions reduce di/dt at the cost of smaller eye opening) and through staggered switching (slightly different timing for each DQ bit reduces the instantaneous total di/dt, though this is limited by the matching requirements).

For layout, the most effective strategy is maximising the decoupling capacitance close to the IO cells and minimising the inductive path to that capacitance. MIM capacitors placed directly over the IO cells, connected through short via stacks to the VDDQ and VSS rails, provide the lowest-inductance decoupling path.

### Q6. How is the PDN resonance managed in LPDDR5 designs?

**Answer:**

PDN resonance occurs when the inductive impedance of the power delivery path (from package bumps through the on-die power grid to the decoupling capacitors) equals the capacitive impedance of the decoupling capacitors. At the resonant frequency, the impedance peaks, potentially exceeding the target impedance and amplifying noise at that frequency.

The resonant frequency is f_res = 1 / (2 * pi * sqrt(L * C)), where L is the effective inductance of the bump + package path and C is the total on-die decoupling capacitance. For typical LPDDR5 values (L = 100-300 pH, C = 100-500 pF), the resonant frequency falls between 400 MHz and 1.6 GHz, which is squarely within the LPDDR5 operating frequency range.

Managing PDN resonance requires damping the resonance peak to keep the impedance below the target. The ESR (Equivalent Series Resistance) of the decoupling capacitors provides natural damping. MIM capacitors have low ESR (10-50 mohm), which means the resonance peak can be sharp and high. Adding intentional resistance (by using MOS or MOM capacitors with higher ESR) broadens and lowers the peak.

Distributing the decoupling across multiple capacitor types with different resonant frequencies flattens the impedance profile. A combination of large MOS capacitors (effective at low frequencies), medium MIM capacitors (effective at mid frequencies), and small MOM capacitors (effective at high frequencies) creates overlapping impedance coverage.

Reducing the inductance shifts the resonant frequency higher (where the target impedance is typically less stringent) and reduces the impedance at all frequencies. This is the most effective approach for LPDDR5.

For layout, resonance management is verified by PDN impedance analysis. The impedance plot should be checked for peaks that exceed Z_target. If peaks are found, the decoupling capacitor mix and placement are adjusted, and the analysis is repeated until the impedance is below the target across the entire frequency range.

### Q7. How does power supply noise affect timing in LPDDR5?

**Answer:**

Power supply noise converts to timing uncertainty through several mechanisms. Supply-induced jitter on clock signals occurs because the PLL/DLL VCO frequency is modulated by power supply noise on its supply rail. A 10 mV noise on a VCO with 1 GHz/V gain sensitivity creates 10 MHz of frequency modulation, which translates to jitter on the output clock. This jitter propagates to all signals timed by that clock.

Driver delay modulation occurs because the propagation delay through the output driver depends on the supply voltage. When VDDQ droops, the PMOS pull-up becomes weaker (less gate overdrive), slowing the rising edge. The delay sensitivity is typically 1-5 ps per mV of supply variation. A 20 mV VDDQ droop creates 20-100 ps of delay variation, which directly shifts the data eye position.

Receiver threshold modulation occurs when VREF tracks VDDQ (if VREF is generated from VDDQ via a resistor divider). A VDDQ droop shifts VREF proportionally, changing the receiver decision threshold. If the VREF generator has filtering (which it should), the VREF shift is attenuated at high frequencies.

The timing impact of power supply noise is often larger than the direct voltage impact. For example, a 20 mV VDDQ noise reduces the voltage margin by 20 mV (8% of the 250 mV ideal eye height), but the associated timing shift of 50 ps may consume 32% of the 156.25 ps UI.

For layout, minimising the timing impact of power supply noise requires isolating the PLL/DLL power supply from the noisy IO VDDQ (using a separate regulator or dedicated power bumps with filtering), filtering the VREF generator power supply, and minimising VDDQ noise at the driver through low-impedance power grid and close decoupling.

### Q8. How is decoupling capacitor effectiveness verified?

**Answer:**

Decoupling capacitor effectiveness is verified by measuring the PDN impedance with and without the capacitors and confirming that the impedance meets the target. The verification is performed using PDN analysis tools (Cadence Voltus, Synopsys RedHawk, ANSYS RedHawk-SC).

The verification flow involves extracting the on-die power grid with all decoupling capacitors included in the model. The extraction captures the capacitance value, ESR, ESL, and physical location of each capacitor. The PDN impedance is computed at each IO cell location across the frequency range. The impedance is compared against the target (Z_target = V_noise / I_max). The analysis is then repeated without decoupling (to quantify the improvement provided by the capacitors) and with reduced or redistributed decoupling (to optimise placement).

Effectiveness metrics include the impedance reduction ratio (impedance with decoupling / impedance without decoupling) at key frequencies, the frequency range over which the capacitor is effective (typically defined as the range where the capacitor contributes more than 3 dB of impedance reduction), the remaining margin (Z_target minus Z_actual) at the worst-case frequency, and the transient voltage droop in time-domain simulation (applying the worst-case current profile and measuring the VDDQ waveform at each IO cell).

Common issues revealed during verification include insufficient capacitance near the farthest IO cells (the cells at the ends of the PHY may be far from the MIM capacitor block), resonance peaks between the package inductance and on-die capacitance, and excessive ESL in the connection from the capacitor to the power grid (long via paths or narrow metal connections).

### Q9. What is the impact of power grid routing on signal routing resources?

**Answer:**

The power grid and signal routing compete for metal resources on the same layers. In LPDDR5 PHY layout, this competition is particularly intense because both the power grid and signal routing have demanding requirements, and the PHY area is compact.

The power grid typically claims the top 2-3 metal layers (M10-M12 in a 12-metal process) for wide VDDQ and VSS straps. These layers provide the lowest resistance and highest current capacity. However, they may also be the preferred layers for signal routing (especially for long-distance signals like the DFI bus).

The signal routing (DQ, DQS, CA, CK, WCK) typically uses the intermediate metal layers (M6-M9). However, these layers may also be needed for local power grid distribution and via connections between the top-layer power grid and the IO cells.

The competition is resolved through careful layer assignment planning during floorplanning. A typical assignment reserves M10-M12 for power grid (VDDQ/VSS horizontal and vertical straps), M8-M9 for DQ/DQS signal routing and CK/WCK routing, M6-M7 for CA signal routing and local connections, and M1-M5 for standard cell internal routing and IO cell internal connections.

Within each layer, the power and signal routing must share the metal resources. A common approach uses a "channel" routing style: power straps run at regular intervals across the routing area, with signal traces routed between the power straps. The power strap pitch determines the available routing width for signals. A wider strap pitch means more signal routing space but weaker power grid.

The layout engineer must balance these competing demands, using IR drop and timing analysis to verify that both power and signal requirements are met simultaneously.

### Q10. How do advanced node effects (FinFET, sub-5nm) affect power integrity for LPDDR?

**Answer:**

Advanced process nodes (5nm, 3nm FinFET) introduce several effects that complicate power integrity for LPDDR interfaces.

Higher current density per area is driven by the trend toward more functionality in smaller die areas. The LPDDR PHY, while physically smaller in advanced nodes, still draws similar total current (because the data rate increases to compensate for the smaller process). The current per unit area increases, requiring denser power grids.

Thinner metal layers in advanced nodes (especially the lower layers) have higher resistance, which increases IR drop for the same metal width. The top metal layers (used for power distribution) may maintain their thickness, but the intermediate layers (used for signal routing and local power distribution) become thinner and more resistive.

Increased transistor leakage in FinFET devices at low threshold voltages adds to the static current demand, particularly in the IO cells where many transistors are used for the segmented driver and ODT circuits. The leakage current contributes to IR drop and thermal dissipation.

Reduced voltage margins as VDDQ continues to decrease (potentially below 0.5V in future nodes), the absolute noise budget shrinks proportionally. This drives more aggressive decoupling and lower-impedance power grid requirements.

Self-heating in FinFET devices (where the fin structure has poor thermal conductivity to the substrate) causes local temperature increases that affect transistor performance. In IO cells with concentrated switching activity, self-heating can increase the driver's on-resistance and reduce the effective impedance calibration accuracy.

For layout, these advanced-node effects require denser power grids with more metal allocated to VDDQ/VSS, more aggressive use of MIM and MOM decoupling to compensate for higher-frequency noise, and careful thermal management to avoid self-heating-induced performance degradation in the IO cells. The power integrity analysis must include advanced-node-specific models that capture FinFET leakage and self-heating effects.
